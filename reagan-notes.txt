President Ronald Reagan’s 1981 and 1986 tax cuts significantly reduced marginal rates for top 
earners and corporations, aligning with supply-side economic theories that reduced burdens would 
stimulate overall production and investment.

📉 The Core Policy Shifts

• Top Individual Rate: Slashed from 70% down to 28% across his administration.
• Corporate Rates: Lowered maximum rates to incentivize private sector growth.
• Supply-Side Rationale: Proponents like the Ronald Reagan Presidential Foundation & Institute 
argue these incentives catalyzed job creation and lowered inflation.
• Inequality Concerns: Critics and historical analyses noted by groups like the Center for Public 
Integrity point to widening income gaps and tripled national debt as negative long-term impacts.

⚖️ Economic Debate Overview

• Supporters' View: Lower marginal rates encouraged investment, helping drop unemployment and inflation.
• Critics' View: Wealth accumulated at the top while national deficits swelled.
• Bipartisan Correction: The later Tax Reform Act of 1986 closed various tax shelters and scaled 
back specific corporate loopholes to balance broader revisions

Reaganomics lowered inflation and created jobs, but it also contributed to long-term wage stagnation, declining union power, and a widening wealth gap for the middle class.

Structural Challenges and Long-Term Pressures
Decline of Labor Unions: President Reagan’s handling of the 1981 PATCO strike signaled a shift in government support for labor unions, leading to a steady decline in private-sector union membership and reduced bargaining power for workers. 

Wage Stagnation: Critics point out that despite overall GDP growth, hourly wages for average production and nonsupervisory workers flattened or lagged behind overall productivity gains. 

Rising Inequality: Tax policies and financial deregulation shifted a larger share of economic gains toward top earners and corporations, contributing to a growing income gap that critics argue began hollowing out the middle class. 

Social Spending Cuts: Reductions in federal funding for domestic and social programs—including education, job training, and assistance programs—removed some pathways of upward mobility for lower-income households trying to enter the middle class.
